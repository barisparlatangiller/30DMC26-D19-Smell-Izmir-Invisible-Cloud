#30DayMapChallenge · Day 19 · Smell · İzmir

The invisible cloud over İzmir

Sewage-fed streams and canals empty into the shallow inner gulf. On a hot July afternoon the imbat, the sea breeze from the north-west, picks up the smell of the water, the stream mouths, a wastewater plant and a landfill and pushes it into the city. Where does it go, hour by hour?

Buildings slow the wind near the ground, so the cloud lingers longer in dense blocks: +6% more odour there on 21 July.

        How to read this map
        1. The haze is the modelled odour cloud near the ground. Its colour shows where it comes from: yellow from the gulf water, orange from the stream and canal mouths, sage from the wastewater plant, mauve from the landfill. Thicker and brighter means stronger.
        2. Symbols mark the sources; a bigger symbol is a stronger source. Small wave symbols are the 11 gulf monitoring stations (hover for the sewage value). Moving streaks show where the air carries the smell this hour, in the same colours. Murky olive water marks where the sewage signal is highest.
        3. Pick a day and an hour. In summer the imbat blows from the north-west in the afternoon; at night the wind drops and the air slides down the slopes to the gulf.
        4. Click anywhere: odour now, odour hours that day, wind and the neighbourhood.
        Method, data & limits

        Wind. Hourly 10 m wind, temperature and humidity for the 21st of every month, Nov 2025 – Oct 2026 (Open-Meteo, one point in the gulf). On the map the wind slows and turns where it would have to climb a steep slope, speeds up over open ridges and the sea, and drains down the slopes on calm nights.

        Buildings. 323,318 OSM footprints. Heights from OSM tags where mapped (282), otherwise from GHSL GHS-BUILT-H 2018 (100 m). In the “with buildings” scenario dense, tall blocks slow the wind near the ground to 40–70% of the open value.

        The gulf. The water near the shore releases odour in proportion to the sewage signal measured by İzmir Metropolitan Municipality at 11 gulf stations (median faecal coliform and E. coli, 2012–2026, about 340 samples per station). The signal is spread over the water by distance and fades 1–2 km offshore; it is highest off Meles, Bayraklı, Karşıyaka pier and Konak. A warmer sea makes it stronger: hourly sea surface temperature from Open-Meteo Marine, from about 0.3× at 14 °C in winter to 1.7× at 25 °C in July. Other sources, relative weights, not emissions: stream and canal mouths that carry sewage overflow (Meles 1.0, six others 0.5–0.8), Çiğli wastewater plant (0.8), Harmandalı landfill (0.8), Karşıyaka transfer station (0.3).

        Model. Particles are released every hour, carried by the wind in 1-minute steps with turbulent spread, and fade over about an hour. The odour index is the particle density per 100 m cell, scaled 0–1 over all days. “Odour hour” = index ≥ 0.15.

        Limits

        This is a scenario: a way to see how wind, slopes and buildings move air. It is not an odour measurement, an H₂S map or a forecast. There are no public emission rates for these sources, so all sources use relative weights.

        One wind point for the whole area, a simple terrain rule instead of a flow model (no CFD), building heights partly estimated. The gulf's smell depends on water temperature, oxygen and algae, which are only proxied here by the sea surface temperature of one model cell. Coliform counts show sewage in the water, not the smell itself: they are used only to say where along the shore the water is dirtiest.

        Terrain: Copernicus DEM GLO-30. Trees are OSM trees, tree rows and points placed in mapped woods and orchards.
